# Business Case: Atoms vs Ashes for a Nuclearelectrica-Style Utility

## Page 1 of 2: Strategic Case

### Executive Position

A national nuclear utility considering SMR deployment needs a defensible way to screen candidate sites before committing scarce engineering, environmental, grid, and licensing resources. Atoms vs Ashes is designed for that early-stage decision: it turns a large, uneven portfolio of coal, brownfield, and industrial locations into an auditable siting shortlist, with each score linked to criteria, assumptions, evidence status, and follow-up actions.

For a company like Nuclearelectrica, the product is not a replacement for formal site licensing, environmental impact assessment, or regulator-led safety review. Its value is earlier in the funnel: it helps the owner decide where to spend serious money, which sites are not worth advancing, which data gaps must be closed, and how to explain the screening logic to executives, partners, government stakeholders, and technical advisers.

### Buyer Problem

Early SMR siting programs usually suffer from four linked problems. First, site information is fragmented across legacy plant records, public datasets, grid studies, hydrology, natural hazards, population data, and industrial-risk registers. Second, candidate locations are often compared using inconsistent judgement, so decisions become hard to defend when a preferred site is challenged. Third, external consultants may produce useful reports, but the knowledge often remains trapped in static deliverables. Fourth, the organisation must move quickly enough to preserve political and commercial momentum while still showing nuclear-grade discipline.

Atoms vs Ashes addresses these problems by making siting screening repeatable, inspectable, and portfolio-wide. It gives the utility a structured decision layer between raw data collection and full formal site investigation.

### Product Fit

The product fits a Nuclearelectrica-style buyer because the buyer has a strategic need to evaluate multiple brownfield or legacy energy locations against nuclear siting constraints. The workflow is especially relevant where an SMR program must compare coal-transition sites, industrial sites, grid-adjacent sites, and locations with varying public-data quality.

The product provides:

- A criteria-driven scoring framework for natural hazards, human-induced risks, radiological planning factors, emergency planning, socioeconomic factors, and related siting dimensions.
- Data-quality flags that distinguish hard evidence from partial data, missing data, assumptions, and logic-only scoring.
- Audit trails and scoring examples that allow reviewers to understand how a score was produced.
- Report-ready outputs that can support executive briefings, board materials, technical workshops, and consultant scopes of work.
- A practical way to prioritize follow-up data purchases, site visits, engineering studies, and regulator-facing preparation.

### Business Benefits

The first benefit is faster narrowing of the site portfolio. Instead of commissioning deep studies on every possible location, the buyer can triage the portfolio into advance, hold, reject, and data-gap categories. That can reduce wasted technical effort and focus scarce expert time on the locations with the strongest combined case.

The second benefit is better governance. Nuclear siting decisions attract scrutiny. A structured scoring product gives management a common language for discussing why one site moved forward and another did not. It also reduces the risk that an apparently attractive site advances because one important risk category was underweighted or undocumented.

The third benefit is improved consultant leverage. Rather than asking external advisers to start from a blank page, the utility can provide a pre-screened portfolio, documented data gaps, and a traceable scoring framework. Consultants then spend more of their time on high-value validation and less on basic portfolio sorting.

The fourth benefit is strategic optionality. If national policy, grid conditions, fuel strategy, heat offtake, public acceptance, or project sequencing changes, the utility can rerun the screening logic and compare scenarios without restarting the whole process.

## Page 2 of 2: Adoption Case

### Recommended Deployment

The recommended first deployment is a 90-day enterprise pilot followed by a full internal deployment. The pilot should use a bounded portfolio of candidate sites and a controlled set of criteria. The goal is not to declare a final licensed site. The goal is to prove that the product can reproduce a credible shortlist, expose data gaps, and support a management decision on which locations deserve deeper work.

The pilot should include three workstreams:

1. Data onboarding: import candidate sites, site metadata, available public datasets, internal evidence supplied by the buyer, and known uncertainty flags.
2. Scoring calibration: review the criteria, thresholds, and weighting assumptions with the buyer's nuclear safety, engineering, environmental, emergency planning, grid, and strategy teams.
3. Decision outputs: produce an executive shortlist, scored site examples, data-gap register, and recommended next-study package.

After the pilot, the full deployment should be hosted in the buyer's controlled environment, with source-code escrow or source access depending on procurement model. The buyer should retain the ability to reproduce any score used in a governance decision.

### Operating Model

The product should be owned by the buyer's SMR program office or strategy function, with technical stewardship from siting, safety, and data teams. It should not sit only with IT, because the value is in the decision model and evidence discipline, not simply in software hosting.

The practical operating model is:

- Program office owns the site portfolio and decision calendar.
- Technical teams validate criteria, evidence, and data-gap priority.
- Data team maintains datasets, connectors, and repeatability.
- External consultants use the outputs as inputs to deeper validation studies.
- Executives use the shortlist and risk narrative for capital allocation and stakeholder decisions.

### Risks and Controls

The main risk is overclaiming. Atoms vs Ashes should be positioned as an early-stage screening and decision-support product, not a licensed site-approval engine. That positioning protects both the vendor and the utility.

The second risk is data incompleteness. This is managed by explicitly labelling partial data, no-data, and logic-only criteria rather than hiding uncertainty inside a single numeric score.

The third risk is organisational adoption. The product will create value only if its outputs are used in real portfolio decisions. The pilot should therefore end with a live decision workshop, not just a software demo.

### Decision Ask

The buyer should approve a paid pilot if it wants to reduce the cost and uncertainty of early SMR siting decisions, create a defensible portfolio-screening record, and improve the quality of consultant and internal engineering work that follows.

The commercial ask should be framed as buying a decision system, not buying a report. The product's value is that the buyer can reuse the framework across site portfolios, scenarios, data refreshes, board reviews, and regulator-preparation cycles.

### Success Measures

A successful first deployment should produce:

- A ranked and categorized portfolio of candidate sites.
- A documented explanation for each advance, hold, reject, or data-gap decision.
- A list of the highest-value missing datasets or studies.
- Evidence that internal experts can challenge and adjust the scoring logic.
- A management-ready recommendation on which sites deserve deeper investment.

If those outcomes are achieved, Atoms vs Ashes becomes a repeatable siting intelligence asset for the buyer rather than a one-off analysis exercise.
